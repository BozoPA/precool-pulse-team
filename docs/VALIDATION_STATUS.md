# Physics identifiability gate — provisional verdict (10 Nov 2026)

**Verdict: AMBER.**
* A limited, software-only proof of concept is plausible and now exists as running code.
* Fault-detection performance in a real precooler is **not proven and must not be claimed**. No independent
  measured dataset with zone-level air readings plus fault labels plus produce temperatures has been found.

Everything below marked *model* is MODEL-GENERATED by `deliverables/prototype` (run of 10 Nov 2026,
63 tests passing). Nothing here is a physical measurement by the team.

## The question
Can local air speed and local supply-to-return temperature rise, read at the return-air face, detect
cooling faults without measuring produce temperature or air mass flow?

## What holds (with the conditions it needs)
1. **The index has a real conservation basis.** If a zone's return air is fully mixed over a known area
   and the sensors read the area-average speed and the mixed temperature, then
   `air density x area x cp x speed x (T_return - T_supply)` equals the sensible heat leaving that zone's
   produce. Tested to 1e-9 relative error in three scenarios (*model*).
2. **Restricted vents and in-carton bypass are detectable in the model.** Zone 5 with 9x resistance: speed
   0.33x, temperature rise 1.24x, UCI 0.41x of the batch median; its produce takes 2.15x as long to reach
   7/8 cooling. Temperature alone misses it, as Phase 1 argued (*model*).

## What does not hold
3. **The multiplied index cannot locate a tarp-gap bypass.** A gap lowers the pressure for all zones
   together, so every zone's produce is starved equally and UCI is identical in all eight zones. What does
   locate the gap is the *pair* (speed up, temperature rise down), not their product; a speed-only or
   temperature-only check locates it equally well. With a 0.10 m2 gap all zones cool 24% slower (*model*).
   Consequence: Phase 1's sentence "multiplied together, they show useful cooling" is correct for the heat
   rate but wrong as a way to find this fault. The claim should be "two signals read side by side".
4. **A small gap raises a loud alarm for almost no harm.** A 0.01 m2 gap doubles zone-1 air speed but slows
   cooling by 2% (*model*). The waste there is fan energy, not cooling time.
5. **Start-of-batch readings cannot separate "bypass" from "cooler produce".** A zone with 40% in-carton
   bypass and produce at 39.4 C gives exactly the same speed and temperature rise as a healthy zone at 27 C,
   yet cools 42% slower; the detector says PASS. In reverse, a healthy pallet that simply starts at 20 C is
   flagged. In general the reading scales with (1 - bypass share) x (produce temperature - supply
   temperature), so two unknowns hide in one number (*model*, and exact algebra).
   What would fix it: a produce pulp temperature for each zone at loading, or watching how fast the
   temperature rise decays over tens of minutes (which is no longer "opening minutes").
6. **Mixing and sensor position decide the answer.** If return air mixes between neighbouring zones the
   wrong zone is also blamed. If the sensor sits outside the bypass jet the bypass is invisible at zone
   level. Only the whole-batch total survives mixing (*model*).
7. **Uniform faults need a stored baseline.** A weak fan or a missed jet is caught only by comparing the
   batch total with a known fault-free run of the same load and start temperature. Without it: PASS.
8. **Speed-sensor accuracy is probably the binding constraint.** Alimardani & Hoff (2005) report velocity
   accuracy of 0.11 to 0.24 m/s, calibrated at 20 to 45 C. The model's face velocity is 0.4 m/s
   (assumption). If each sensor carries a fixed unknown offset of that size, healthy batches raise a false
   alarm in 90% (0.11 m/s) to 99% (0.24 m/s) of runs; at 0.05 m/s, 32%; at 0.02 m/s, 0% (*model*, 200 runs
   each). The Phase 1 gate of at most 3 false alarms in 30 normal runs would need zone-to-zone agreement
   near 0.02 m/s, or sensors placed where air moves much faster, or per-zone baselines that stay stable.

## Not modelled yet (each could change the picture)
Evaporative cooling and humidity; respiration heat; carton and pallet thermal mass; fan heat and room gains;
temperature variation inside a fruit; 2-D/3-D airflow; start-up transients when the fan switches on;
velocity sensors responding to speed regardless of direction; sensor behaviour in cold humid air.

# Update — 11 Nov 2026 (milestone 2). Verdict unchanged: AMBER.

## Level C: model against published measurements (partial)
Data: Sadie et al. (2025), Zenodo doi:10.5281/zenodo.15726691, CC BY 4.0. Valencia oranges in a 1.2 m deep
laboratory cooling tunnel at 0.05, 0.20 and 0.35 m/s; two pulp probes per tunnel; 10-minute readings for 26 h.
These are PUBLISHED MEASURED values. The model columns are MODEL-GENERATED with no rate parameter fitted.

| Air speed | Half-cooling time, two probes (measured) | Model: inlet / bed average / outlet | Bed average vs probe average |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 m/s | 3.30 h and 5.15 h | 1.39 / 5.87 / 11.31 h | +39% |
| 0.20 m/s | 1.34 h and 1.93 h | 0.65 / 1.70 / 3.05 h | +4% |
| 0.35 m/s | 1.06 h and 1.44 h | 0.49 / 1.08 / 1.84 h | −14% |

* Both probes fall inside the model's inlet-to-outlet range at all three speeds, for half- and 7/8-cooling
  times. That range is wide (a factor of 4 to 8), so this is a weak test.
* 7/8-cooling time, bed average vs probe average: +20%, −4%, −16%.
* The model exaggerates the effect of air speed: 5.4x between 0.05 and 0.35 m/s against 3.4x measured. It has
  no natural convection or conduction, which matter at 0.05 m/s.
* Taken from the experiment: air speed, bed depth, start temperature, and the air temperature (the fruit's
  final temperature, about 0 C; the room set point was −0.6 C). With the set point instead, half-cooling
  times still fall in range but 7/8 times at 0.20 and 0.35 m/s do not.
* Assumed because the paper does not report them: bulk density 540 kg/m3, fruit diameter 0.08 m, probe
  positions along the flow. Moving bulk density by 15% moves the errors by about 10 to 18 points
  (`outputs/benchmark_sensitivity.csv`).
* **Scope.** This supports the produce-cooling part of the model for one fruit in one laboratory. It says
  nothing about the airflow network, the sensor rail or the detector. Level D is still unavailable.

## A measured fact that sharpens finding 8
The same paper reports superficial air speeds through pallets at four commercial citrus precoolers:
0.14, 0.17, 0.19 and 0.27 m/s (PUBLISHED MEASURED, derived from pressure drop). The cited thermistor
anemometer's accuracy is 0.11 to 0.24 m/s. At a return face the error could be as large as the signal.
The speed sensor, not the algorithm, is the first thing a physical prototype must prove.

## Evaporation (new, crude)
With an illustrative transpiration coefficient (5e-10 kg/kg/s/Pa, not from a verified source), 11.9% of the
heat leaves the produce as water vapour, which air-temperature sensors cannot see; cooling is 8% faster and no
alarm is raised (*model*). UCI therefore under-reads true heat removal by a crop-dependent amount. It still
works for comparing zones of the same crop, not as an energy meter.

## Pulp temperature per zone (new input; a change from the Phase 1 design)
If the operator probes one fruit per zone at loading and the detector divides heat pickup by
(pulp − supply temperature):
* the hidden-bypass twin (finding 5) is flagged, and the cool-pallet false alarm disappears (*model*);
* it tolerates a pulp reading error up to about 2 K (false alarms 1%); at 3 K false alarms reach 15% (*model*,
  200 runs);
* it does not help with a missed jet or a weak fan; those still need a stored baseline.
Cost of the change: eight probe readings per batch, or eight more sensors. This must be disclosed as a
design change if adopted.

# Update — 11 Nov 2026 (after the independent role reviews). Verdict unchanged: AMBER.
* The whole-batch check now compares with an **earlier** fault-free batch, not with the batch being judged.
  That exposes one more limit of the Phase 1 design: a healthy batch that starts cooler than the stored one
  raises a false BATCH LOW. With a pulp temperature per zone it does not (*model*, tested).
* A short run can no longer hide a slow zone, and unstable time steps are refused.
* Scenario results and all published numbers are unchanged by these fixes.
* No human expert has reviewed the physics. That remains the largest gap in the review record.
